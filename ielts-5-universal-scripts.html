How to Use These Scripts

Each Part 2 script is built around a core story with multiple "angles" you can foreground depending on the cue card. Don't memorise word-for-word — instead, internalise the chunks so you can recombine them naturally on the day.

Step 1. Identify which of the 5 scripts your cue card maps to (use the coverage tags).

Step 2. Select 2–3 paragraphs most relevant to the four bullet points on the cue card.

Step 3. Open with a flexible lead, then deliver those paragraphs in order, weaving in the cue card's specific wording.

For Part 3, the 5 thematic banks give you ready-made arguments and Band 7 collocations using a Position → Reason → Example → Counterpoint structure.

Part 2 — The Five Universal Stories

Script 1 · My Father

Theme: A versatile, admirable mentor figure
Cue cards this script covers (7)
  • A person who often helps others
  • A person who encouraged you to protect nature
  • A person who solved a problem in a smart way
  • A person who makes plans a lot
  • A time you felt proud of a family member
  • A time you encouraged someone to do something
  • A time you gave advice to others
Adaptation tip: Open with a sentence tailored to the cue (e.g. "…the most helpful person I know" / "…a meticulous planner" / "…incredibly resourceful"). Then pick 2–3 paragraphs.

The person I'd like to describe is my father, Mr. Chen, who has undoubtedly been the most influential figure in my life. He's a civil engineer in his early fifties, and what really makes him stand out is this rare blend of meticulous planning, problem-solving ingenuity, and a genuinely compassionate nature.

[Helper angle] Take his approach to helping others — he's the go-to person in our entire neighbourhood. Whenever a pipe bursts or an elderly resident needs help filing paperwork, my father is the first to roll up his sleeves. He doesn't help reluctantly out of obligation; rather, he genuinely takes pleasure in lending a hand, which I find deeply admirable.

[Smart problem-solver angle] What's equally remarkable is how he tackles challenges. About two years ago, our community faced serious flooding every monsoon season. While most residents merely complained, my father quietly studied the drainage system, drafted a proposal, and persuaded the local authorities to install permeable pavements — a low-cost fix that spared the neighbourhood years of frustration. It was an incredibly resourceful solution that nobody else had even contemplated.

[Planner angle] He's also an obsessive planner — and I mean that in the best possible way. Every Sunday evening, he sits down with his leather-bound notebook and maps out the week ahead, from work deadlines to family activities. He even plans our holidays a year in advance — which used to drive me crazy as a teenager, but now I see how it gives our family a sense of structure and anticipation.

[Nature angle] On top of that, he's deeply passionate about environmental protection. Growing up, he constantly encouraged me to cut down on waste, separate recycling diligently, and appreciate the natural world. He'd take me hiking on weekends and point out how human activity was reshaping the landscape — those quiet conversations planted the seeds of environmental awareness in me, and I still recycle religiously to this day.

[Encourager / Advice-giver angle] There was also a time he gently encouraged me to do something I was dreading — applying to a competitive university overseas. I was convinced I'd be rejected, but he sat me down and gave me what turned out to be life-changing advice: that fear of rejection is rarely a good reason to avoid trying. He didn't pressure me — he simply outlined the pros and cons clearly and let me decide. I applied, got in, and it transformed my life.

[Pride angle] What truly made me proud of him was how he handled my grandmother's illness last year. He coordinated her treatment, comforted the rest of the family, and managed his work simultaneously — all without a word of complaint. That moment crystallised for me what real strength and integrity look like. Honestly, he isn't just my father; he's my role model.

Script 2 · My Cousin Lily

Theme: A creative, self-driven young person
Cue cards this script covers (4)
  • A child who likes drawing very much
  • A friend who learned something without a teacher
  • A famous person you would like to meet (Lily's idol → yours too)
  • A time you needed to use your imagination
Adaptation tip: If the cue is "use your imagination," reframe so you were the one imagining, with Lily as inspiration. For "famous person," lead with the Miyazaki paragraph.

I'd like to talk about my younger cousin Lily, who is one of the most creative and self-driven people I know. She's twelve years old, and she's been completely obsessed with drawing for as long as I can remember.

[Drawing child angle] I first noticed her talent when she was barely five — she'd cover entire walls with sketches of dragons, fairies, and imaginary creatures. What struck me was that she wasn't copying anyone; the worlds she created came entirely from her own mind. Today, she draws every single day, often spending three to four hours after school refining her digital illustrations. I genuinely believe she's drawn to art because it gives her a private universe where rules don't apply — a sanctuary of pure expression.

[Self-learner angle] The remarkable thing is, she's almost entirely self-taught. Our parents never enrolled her in formal art classes — she simply picked up everything through YouTube tutorials, online communities, and sheer determination. She mastered Procreate on her iPad when she was nine, and within a year, her work had reached a level that left adults astonished. Honestly, I think learning on her own gave her a distinctive stylistic voice that no traditional class would have nurtured. Of course, having a teacher might have accelerated the technical side, but the originality? That's purely hers.

[Famous person angle] Lily idolises Hayao Miyazaki, the legendary Japanese animator behind Studio Ghibli films. If she — or I — could meet anyone in the world, it would absolutely be him. We both admire not just his artistic vision but the way he weaves profound themes like environmentalism and the loss of innocence into stories children can love. Ideally, I'd love to meet him at the Studio Ghibli Museum in Tokyo and simply watch him sketch — I think you can learn more from observing a master at work than from any interview.

[Imagination angle] Watching Lily has actually pushed me to flex my own imagination. Last summer, she invited me to co-design an entirely fictional kingdom — complete with maps, characters, languages, even political systems. I had to invent backstories, dream up landscapes, and devise a writing system overnight. It was surprisingly difficult at first because, as adults, we get so used to dealing with concrete, practical problems; pure imagination feels almost like a forgotten muscle. But once I let go of the urge to be "realistic," ideas started flowing thick and fast. It was genuinely exhilarating.

Overall, I admire Lily for staying so passionate and self-motivated. In an age of endless distractions, her dedication is something quite extraordinary, and I think she's going to make a real mark on the creative world one day.

Script 3 · A Road Trip Through Yunnan

Theme: A multi-purpose travel anecdote
Cue cards this script covers (5)
  • A bicycle / motorcycle / car trip you would like to go on
  • An event where you didn't enjoy the music played
  • A foreign country you would like to stay in for a short period
  • An occasion when many people were smiling
  • An occasion when you were not allowed to use your mobile phone
Adaptation tip: Treat each paragraph as a "module." If the cue is "no-phone occasion," lead with the Songzanlin Monastery section. If it's "foreign country," swap in the Hokkaido paragraph as the centrepiece.

I'd like to describe a memorable road trip I took to Yunnan Province last summer with three of my closest university friends. We'd been planning this getaway for months, dreaming of driving across the breathtaking landscapes of southwestern China — rolling tea plantations, ancient towns, and snow-capped mountains.

[Car / road trip angle] We chose to travel by car for several reasons. Firstly, public transport simply couldn't reach the remote villages we wanted to explore. Secondly, having our own vehicle gave us complete freedom to stop wherever we pleased — whether that was to photograph a rice terrace at sunrise or sample a roadside noodle stall. We rented an SUV and clocked over 1,500 kilometres in ten days. Compared with flying or taking the train, driving forced us to slow down and savour the journey itself, not just the destination.

[Foreign country angle] If I could pick a similar adventure abroad, I'd absolutely choose Japan, particularly Hokkaido. I first got to know the region through travel vlogs and Studio Ghibli landscapes — its lavender fields and snowy onsen towns have been on my bucket list for years. A short stay there — say, three weeks — would let me experience the cuisine, language, and rural culture without overwhelming homesickness or burning through my savings. I'd cycle around Furano, try local seafood in Hakodate, and visit small bookshops in Sapporo. The reason it would be short is honestly practical: I'm still studying, and a longer stay would interfere with coursework.

[Smiling occasion angle] One unforgettable moment in Yunnan was visiting Lijiang Old Town's Sunday market. The whole square was teeming with locals, tourists, performers, and food vendors — and almost everyone was smiling. Children were chasing balloons, an elderly couple was dancing to traditional Naxi music, and travellers were striking up conversations with strangers. I think people were smiling because the atmosphere was wonderfully unpretentious — nobody was rushing, nobody was glued to their phone, and there was this collective sense that we'd all stepped into a slower, kinder version of life.

[Disappointing music angle] Not everything went smoothly, though. We attended a so-called "cultural music festival" in Dali that I really didn't enjoy. It had been advertised as authentic ethnic minority folk music, but it turned out to be a loud, over-amplified pop concert with synthesiser beats drowning out the traditional instruments. The acoustics were dreadful, and the crowd was so densely packed that we couldn't even see the stage. I left feeling thoroughly disappointed, partly because I'd been genuinely curious about the region's musical heritage, and partly because it felt like the organisers were cashing in on culture rather than honouring it.

[No-phone occasion angle] Another moment worth mentioning was our visit to Songzanlin Monastery in Shangri-La. Phones were strictly prohibited inside — we had to leave them at the entrance. At first I felt uneasy, almost naked without my device, and I kept reaching for my pocket out of habit. But within an hour I realised how peaceful it was. We just observed, prayed, and spoke with the monks. It was honestly the most mindful and meaningful hour of the entire trip — and it made me question how much our phones quietly steal from our experiences.

Looking back, that journey taught me that the best travel happens when you slow down and engage with the moment — whether that's behind the wheel of a car or sitting silently in an ancient temple.

Script 4 · TX Huaihai & My Reading Nook

Theme: A two-in-one place script
Cue cards this script covers (3)
  • An interesting building
  • A shopping mall
  • Your favourite place at home where you can relax
Adaptation tip: The first half is about TX Huaihai (covers building + mall). The second half pivots to your reading nook at home. Use whichever half — or both — depending on the prompt.

I'd like to describe a place that's both architecturally fascinating and personally meaningful to me — it's TX Huaihai, a cultural shopping complex in central Shanghai. And I'll also touch on my own reading corner at home, since the two complement each other.

[Interesting building angle] TX Huaihai is unlike any conventional mall you've ever seen. Located on Huaihai Middle Road, the building features a striking minimalist exterior with raw concrete walls, exposed steel beams, and oversized geometric windows. The architects clearly drew inspiration from old industrial warehouses but reimagined them as a vibrant cultural hub. What makes it genuinely interesting is how it blurs the line between retail, art, and community space — it almost feels more like a museum than a mall.

[Mall angle — function & visits] Inside, instead of the usual chain stores, you'll find independent designer brands, niche perfumeries, vinyl record shops, art galleries, and pop-up exhibitions that change every few weeks. I visit it about once a month, mainly because I love browsing the bookshop on the third floor and grabbing a flat white at one of the specialty cafés tucked inside. I rarely spend a fortune there — usually I just pick up small things like stationery, an indie magazine, or a limited-edition vinyl. The function of the building actually extends far beyond shopping — it serves as a creative gathering point for young Shanghainese, hosting talks, film screenings, and even small concerts.

I think TX Huaihai represents a new wave of urban design — one that prioritises experience and culture over pure consumption. That's exactly why I find it so worth talking about.

[Relaxing place at home angle] Now, when I genuinely want to switch off and decompress, my favourite spot is actually a small reading nook I've created in my bedroom. It's tucked beside a tall window with a soft beige armchair, a low wooden side table, and shelves crammed with novels and travel guides. I drape a knitted throw over the chair, light a sandalwood candle, and just lose myself in a book or my journal.

I usually spend an hour there every evening — sometimes reading, sometimes just watching the city lights flicker through the curtain. The reason I find it so relaxing is that it's a complete sensory escape. The natural light by day, the warm glow at night, the faint smell of the candle, the silence broken only by birdsong outside — it's the perfect antidote to the chaos of city life. After a long, draining day, even twenty minutes there can completely reset my mind. I genuinely believe everyone needs a small sanctuary like this — somewhere life can't reach you for a while.

Script 5 · My Vintage Camera & Films

Theme: A meaningful object linking media, family, career & daily life
Cue cards this script covers (8)
  • An item on which you spent more than expected
  • A movie you watched and enjoyed recently
  • Something important kept in your family for a long time
  • A story you have read recently
  • A program or app on your computer or phone
  • Something you can't live without (not phone/computer)
  • Your perfect job
  • A movie you felt disappointed about
Adaptation tip: The most modular script. Pick 2–3 paragraphs matching the cue card and lead with that paragraph's opening sentence as your topic sentence. The camera is the "anchor" tying everything together.

I'd like to talk about something deeply meaningful to me — my grandfather's vintage Leica film camera, which has become a kind of bridge between my passion for storytelling and my career aspirations.

[Family heirloom angle] The camera was made in the 1970s. My grandfather, who worked as a journalist, used it for decades to document everything from family birthdays to historic events. When he passed away three years ago, my father handed it down to me. It's something our family has cherished for nearly fifty years, and now it sits proudly on my desk — not just as a tool, but as a tangible piece of family history. Every scratch on the body tells a story.

[Overspending angle] When I decided to restore and use it actively, I ended up spending way more than I'd anticipated. Originally I'd budgeted around 1,000 yuan for film and a basic service, but I ended up paying nearly 5,000 yuan once I factored in lens repairs, film stocks, scanning equipment, and a darkroom subscription. I overspent because, frankly, I'd underestimated the true cost of analogue photography — every roll of film is essentially a luxury item now. Honestly, though, I don't regret a single yuan; I see it as an investment in something irreplaceable.

[Can't-live-without angle] I genuinely can't imagine life without this camera. While most people would say their phone is essential, for me it's the camera. Carrying it forces me to slow down, observe the world, and capture moments deliberately. It's grounded my mental health more than any meditation app ever has — there's something almost meditative about composing a single frame and waiting two weeks to see the result.

[App angle] Speaking of apps, I do rely heavily on Halide and VSCO on my phone for digital photography and editing. I discovered Halide through a YouTube review and I've used it almost daily for two years. What I love about it is its intuitive interface and professional-grade controls — it basically transforms my phone into a serious camera. I'd say apps like this have made photography far more accessible without sacrificing quality.

[Movie enjoyed angle] My passion for visual storytelling extends to films. Recently, I rewatched The Secret Life of Walter Mitty, and I enjoyed it just as much as the first time. I watched it alone at home one rainy weekend in autumn. The cinematography is absolutely breathtaking, and the protagonist's journey resonated deeply — it's about finding the courage to live life fully rather than just imagining it. I rewatched it because I needed inspiration during a stressful exam period, and it delivered exactly that.

[Disappointing movie angle] That said, not every film hits the mark. I recently watched a heavily marketed Chinese sci-fi blockbuster that left me thoroughly underwhelmed. I'd gone because the trailer looked spectacular and the early reviews were glowing, but the plot turned out to be incoherent, the characters were paper-thin, and it relied almost entirely on visual effects to mask a hollow story. I left the cinema feeling cheated, and it made me far more sceptical of hyped-up reviews and aggressive marketing.

[Story read recently angle] I also recently read a short story called The Story of an Hour by Kate Chopin. It's a haunting tale about a woman's brief, secret moment of perceived freedom after hearing her husband has died. I read it on my commute, and it deeply moved me — it taught me how a single hour can contain a lifetime of emotion, and how literature can compress vast feelings into a few hundred words.

[Perfect job angle] All of this leads to my dream career — to become a documentary filmmaker or photojournalist. To me, that would be the perfect job because it combines storytelling, travel, and visual art into a single profession. To get there, I'd need to learn cinematography, master editing software like DaVinci Resolve, and develop strong interview and research skills. I'd also need patience and emotional intelligence to capture authentic human stories. I first heard about this path from a Vimeo documentary that completely changed how I saw the medium. It's not an easy career — but it's deeply meaningful work, and I'm genuinely working towards it, one frame at a time.

Part 3 — Five Thematic Answer Banks

Part 3 · Bank 1 — Help, Encouragement, Advice, Plans, Pride

Pairs with Part 2 Script 1 (My Father)
Question topics covered: 乐于助人 · 鼓励保护自然 · 机智解决问题 · 常做计划 · 让你骄傲家人 · 鼓励别人 · 给别人建议
Q1. Should children help their parents with household chores? helping
Absolutely — I'd say it's essential rather than optional. Helping with chores instils a sense of responsibility from an early age and teaches kids that family is a shared endeavour. It also cultivates practical life skills — cooking, tidying, basic budgeting — that they'll inevitably need as adults. That said, parents should be careful not to overload them; the chores must be age-appropriate and balanced with study and play, otherwise it could feel more like punishment than participation.
Q2. Why are some people more willing to help others than others? helping
I think it largely comes down to upbringing and personal values. Those raised in supportive, generous households tend to internalise empathy as a default response, whereas people who grew up in highly competitive environments may instinctively guard their own resources. Personality also matters — naturally extroverted, optimistic individuals often find helping rewarding rather than draining. And past experience plays a huge role: those who've received kindness in tough times are usually the first to pay it forward.
Q3. Is it important to plan ahead? planning
I'd say it's crucial, especially in today's fast-paced world. Without a roadmap, it's easy to drift aimlessly or be overwhelmed by competing demands. Good planning provides direction, reduces decision fatigue, and helps you anticipate setbacks before they spiral. However, there's a balance to strike — over-planning can stifle spontaneity and adaptability, both of which are equally valuable. Personally, I plan the structure of my week but leave my weekends loose.
Q4. Should children plan their future careers? planning
To some extent, yes, but cautiously. Encouraging children to think about their interests and strengths is healthy and motivates them to work hard. But expecting them to commit to a specific career at age ten or twelve is unrealistic — their preferences will inevitably evolve as they're exposed to new fields. The far more useful skill is teaching them how to plan and adapt, rather than locking them into a fixed path. The labour market itself is changing too quickly for rigid plans to make sense.
Q5. What's the difference between encouraging someone and pressuring someone? encouragement
That's a subtle but vital distinction. Encouragement comes from a place of belief in someone's potential and respects their autonomy — you offer support, but ultimately let them choose. Pressure, on the other hand, often involves expectations, guilt, or even threats — it strips away the person's agency. Put simply, encouragement empowers; pressure suffocates. The litmus test is how you react when the person says no. If you can accept that gracefully, you were encouraging.
Q6. How can leaders encourage their employees? encouragement
Effective leaders use a blend of recognition, autonomy, and growth opportunities. Publicly acknowledging good work — even with a simple thank-you — can be hugely motivating, because people fundamentally want to feel seen. Secondly, granting employees ownership over their projects builds confidence and signals trust. And thirdly, providing training, mentorship, or stretch assignments shows the company is genuinely invested in their long-term growth — far more powerful than any one-off bonus.
Q7. Should people prepare before giving advice? advice
Definitely. Giving unsolicited or uninformed advice can do more harm than good. Before offering guidance, you should fully understand the situation, listen carefully, and honestly consider whether you're qualified to weigh in. Otherwise, your suggestions may be irrelevant or even misleading. The best advisors ask thoughtful questions before they speak — they treat advice as a dialogue, not a monologue.
Q8. Which is more helpful: advice from older people or from friends of the same age? advice
Both have their merits and the answer really depends on the issue at hand. Older people bring life experience, perspective, and a longer view — they've already navigated dilemmas you're facing for the first time. Peers, however, share your cultural context — they understand modern dating, workplace dynamics, or technology in a way older generations might not. Ideally, you'd triangulate input from both, then make your own call.
Q9. How can helping others benefit the helper? helping
There are real psychological and social benefits, well-documented in research. Numerous studies have shown that altruistic behaviour triggers a "helper's high" — a release of endorphins that boosts mood. It also strengthens social bonds, enhances self-esteem and a sense of purpose, and even correlates with longer life expectancy. So in many ways, helping others is also a form of self-care — counter-intuitive, but true.
Q10. On what occasions would adults be proud of themselves? pride
I think adults tend to feel proud during milestone moments — landing a tough promotion, finishing a marathon, buying their first home, or seeing their children flourish. But equally meaningful are the quieter, everyday wins: standing up for a friend, mastering a difficult skill, or simply staying patient under pressure. The deepest pride, in my view, comes from acts of moral courage rather than external achievements.

Part 3 · Bank 2 — Children, Creativity, Imagination, Fame, Self-learning

Pairs with Part 2 Script 2 (My Cousin Lily)
Question topics covered: 喜欢画画孩子 · 想见名人 · 自学朋友 · 发挥想象力
Q1. What is the right age for a child to learn drawing? drawing
Honestly, I don't think there's a fixed "right age" — most children start scribbling spontaneously around age two or three, and that early exploration is invaluable. Formal lessons probably make sense from around six or seven, when their fine motor skills and attention spans can sustain structured practice. Before that, the focus should be on play-based exposure — letting them experiment with crayons, watercolour, and finger paint without judgment.
Q2. Why do most children draw more often than adults do? drawing
A few reasons. Firstly, children have fewer inhibitions — they don't worry about whether their work is "good," they just create. Adults, sadly, become paralysed by self-criticism. Secondly, children have more unstructured time and unfiltered curiosity, while adults are tied up with work and responsibilities. And thirdly, drawing is genuinely how children make sense of the world — for adults, language has largely replaced that function.
Q3. Should drawing be a compulsory subject in primary school? drawing
Yes, I'd strongly support that. Drawing isn't just about producing pretty pictures — it develops visual literacy, fine motor coordination, and creative problem-solving, all of which transfer to other subjects. It also gives children a non-verbal outlet for emotions they can't yet articulate. The only caveat is that schools must avoid grading it harshly, because the moment art becomes performance-based, kids start losing the joy that makes it valuable in the first place.
Q4. Is it necessary to keep learning after graduating from school? self-learning
Absolutely — I'd argue it's non-negotiable in today's economy. Industries are evolving so rapidly that the skills you graduate with may be partly obsolete within a decade. Lifelong learning isn't just about career survival, though; it keeps the mind sharp, opens up new social circles, and prevents the intellectual stagnation that often creeps in with age. Whether through online courses, reading, or hobbies, learning should never feel like something you "finish."
Q5. Is it better to focus on a few subjects or learn many subjects? self-learning
Both have value, and ideally schooling should combine them. A broad foundation in many subjects gives students cultural literacy, exposes them to different ways of thinking, and helps them discover their genuine interests. But at some point, specialisation matters — depth, not just breadth, is what creates expertise and meaningful contribution. The current trend in education leans toward "T-shaped" learners: broad knowledge across many areas, with deep mastery in one or two.
Q6. Are children born smart, or do they learn to become smart? creativity
It's almost certainly a combination of both — what psychologists call the "nature-versus-nurture" interplay. Genetics may set a baseline for things like processing speed or memory, but environment plays an enormous role. A child raised with rich linguistic input, encouragement, and intellectual stimulation will almost always outperform a more "naturally gifted" child raised in deprivation. So innate ability matters, but it's only the starting point — effort and environment do most of the work.
Q7. Why do some children seem to have more imagination than others? imagination
A lot of it relates to how much freedom they're given. Children whose parents read to them frequently, allow open-ended play, and limit screen time tend to develop richer imaginations because their brains have been trained to fill in the gaps. By contrast, children fed a constant stream of pre-packaged content rarely need to invent anything for themselves. Personality matters too — introverted children often spend more time in their inner world, which can deepen imaginative thinking.
Q8. What kinds of jobs need imagination? imagination
Far more than people realise. Obvious examples include creative industries — writers, designers, filmmakers, advertisers — but I'd argue scientists, engineers, and entrepreneurs are equally dependent on imagination. Theoretical physicists, for instance, have to visualise phenomena that can never be directly observed. Even teachers and therapists rely on imagination to step into someone else's perspective. Honestly, any job that requires innovation needs it.
Q9. Does modern technology help or hurt people's imagination? imagination
It genuinely cuts both ways. On one hand, technology gives people unprecedented tools to externalise their ideas — design software, AI collaborators, virtual worlds. On the other, the passive consumption of short-form video and algorithmic feeds can dull creative thinking, because the work of imagining is being outsourced to the screen. The healthiest approach is using technology as a tool, not a substitute — create with it, don't just consume through it.
Q10. Has the definition of "fame" changed in the age of social media? fame
Drastically. Traditional fame was earned through long apprenticeship in a craft — actors, scientists, athletes who proved themselves over years. Social media fame, by contrast, can be manufactured overnight through viral content, often with no underlying talent. This has made fame far more accessible, but also far more disposable and shallow. The half-life of an internet celebrity is sometimes a matter of weeks, whereas figures like Einstein or Hepburn remain iconic decades later.
Q11. Do people become famous because of their talent? fame
Talent certainly helps, but it's rarely sufficient on its own. Becoming famous usually requires a combination of talent, timing, persistence, and visibility. Plenty of remarkably talented people remain obscure because they lack a platform or the right connections, while less gifted but well-marketed individuals can dominate headlines. So I'd say talent is the foundation, but fame is built on top of it through a mixture of luck, hard work, and self-promotion.

Part 3 · Bank 3 — Travel, Transport, Music, Smiles, Phones

Pairs with Part 2 Script 3 (Yunnan Road Trip)
Question topics covered: 自行车/汽车旅行 · 不喜欢音乐活动 · 外国短期停留 · 微笑场合 · 禁用手机场合
Q1. Do you think air pollution comes mostly from mobile vehicles? transport
Vehicles are a major contributor, but certainly not the only one. In big cities, exhaust emissions can account for a substantial share of urban air pollution — particularly for nitrogen oxides and fine particulates. However, factories, coal-fired power plants, and even agricultural activities also play significant roles. So the most effective response isn't just curbing private car use — it requires a coordinated push toward cleaner energy across all sectors.
Q2. What are the benefits of travelling by bicycle compared with by car? transport
Cycling has clear advantages on multiple fronts. Environmentally, it produces zero emissions; physically, it's excellent low-impact exercise; and economically, it's far cheaper. There's also a more subtle benefit — cycling forces you to engage with your surroundings, noticing details a driver would speed past. The trade-off is obviously range and weather, so cycling works best for short urban trips rather than cross-country journeys.
Q3. What are the benefits of living in a foreign country for a short time? travel
A short stay abroad can be genuinely transformative. You're forced out of your comfort zone, which sharpens problem-solving skills and adaptability. You also gain first-hand cultural insight that no documentary or book can replicate — small things like eating habits, body language, and humour. And socially, it broadens your worldview and often leads to lifelong friendships. The "short" timeframe is actually a feature, not a bug — it concentrates the learning curve.
Q4. Is it better to travel alone or with friends? travel
It really comes down to what you want from the trip. Travelling alone offers freedom, deep self-reflection, and forces you to interact with locals — you grow in ways that are hard to replicate. Travelling with friends, on the other hand, gives you shared memories, safety in numbers, and the comfort of familiar company when things go wrong. Personally, I'd choose solo travel for cultural immersion and group travel for adventure or nightlife.
Q5. How important is music in creating the atmosphere at a social event? music
I'd say music is absolutely central to the emotional fabric of any social event. The right soundtrack can transform a dull gathering into something memorable — it sets the energy, signals transitions, and even primes people's emotions in subtle ways. A good DJ at a wedding can carry guests from teary nostalgia to ecstatic dancing within minutes. Conversely, badly chosen or excessively loud music can ruin the mood entirely.
Q6. Will live music be replaced by recorded music in the future? music
I really don't think so. Recorded music has of course transformed how we consume music day-to-day, but live performance offers something fundamentally different — energy, spontaneity, and a shared experience between artist and audience. People crave that communal, unrepeatable feeling, which is precisely why concert tickets keep selling out at premium prices. If anything, live music has become even more valuable in a digital age.
Q7. Why do most people smile in photographs? smiles
Partly cultural conditioning — we're taught from childhood that smiling looks "presentable" — and partly because photographs freeze a moment in time, and we naturally want that frozen moment to reflect happiness rather than indifference. There's also a social dimension: a smile signals friendliness, approachability, and emotional warmth, which is how most people want to be remembered. Curiously, this convention is fairly modern; in 19th-century portraits, people rarely smiled.
Q8. Can smiling actually make people feel happier? smiles
Yes — there's compelling research behind this, known as the "facial feedback hypothesis". The act of smiling, even when forced, can subtly trigger the release of endorphins and serotonin, marginally lifting your mood. It's not a cure for genuine sadness, but in low-stakes moments, choosing to smile really can nudge your emotional state upward. Combined with the social effect — smiles invite warmer responses from others — the impact compounds.
Q9. Why do some people feel anxious when they cannot use their phones? phones
It's a phenomenon called "nomophobia" — the fear of being without a mobile phone — and it's surprisingly common. Phones have become an extension of our identity: they hold our contacts, payments, photos, and entertainment. Being separated from them feels like losing a security blanket. There's also a deeper issue — many of us use phones to avoid uncomfortable silence or solitude, so removing the device exposes anxieties we'd been distracting ourselves from.
Q10. How can people reduce their screen time? phones
There are several effective strategies. The most powerful is redesigning your environment — leaving the phone in another room while you sleep, or switching it to greyscale, which makes the apps far less seductive. Setting specific phone-free windows — meals, the first hour of the morning — is also remarkably effective. And on the substitution side, having a satisfying offline alternative ready, like a book or a hobby, helps fill the void that scrolling used to occupy.

Part 3 · Bank 4 — Buildings, Shopping, Relaxation

Pairs with Part 2 Script 4 (TX Huaihai & Reading Nook)
Question topics covered: 有趣建筑 · 商场 · 家里放松地方
Q1. Is it worth spending a lot of money on the exterior of a building? architecture
In many cases, yes. A building's exterior is its public face — it shapes how the city feels and signals what the institution stands for. Iconic exteriors like the Sydney Opera House have become entire cities' brand identities. That said, beauty shouldn't come at the expense of usability or sustainability. The best buildings strike a balance between aesthetics and function — neither pure spectacle nor a soulless box.
Q2. Why do people like to visit historical sites? architecture
Historical sites offer something modern attractions can't — a tangible link to the past. Standing inside a thousand-year-old temple connects you to generations of human experience in a way no documentary can. Many people are also drawn to the aesthetic and craftsmanship of old buildings, which often surpasses anything we build today. And practically speaking, these sites help us understand how our ancestors lived, governed, and worshipped.
Q3. What are the challenges in preserving old buildings? architecture
Preservation is a constant balancing act. Financially, restoration is enormously expensive — sourcing original materials and skilled craftsmen often costs more than building from scratch. There's also the tension between authenticity and modern needs: how do you add plumbing or accessibility ramps without compromising historical integrity? And finally, urban development pressure is relentless — old buildings often sit on prime land that developers want to repurpose for high-rises.
Q4. Why do people buy things they don't need? shopping
A combination of psychological and social drivers. Shopping releases dopamine, so it functions as a quick mood boost — what people call "retail therapy." Advertising and social media constantly manufacture artificial desires, making us feel inadequate without the latest product. There's also a status element: people buy things to signal identity or belonging. Recognising these drivers is the first step to spending more intentionally.
Q5. What are the advantages of shopping in a mall compared with online? shopping
Malls offer a multi-sensory experience that online shopping simply can't match. You can try clothes on, feel fabric textures, test electronics, and assess fit before buying — which dramatically reduces returns. Malls are also social spaces: people come for entertainment, dining, and time with family, not just to buy things. Online shopping wins on convenience and price, but malls deliver experience, immediacy, and human connection.
Q6. How can a shopping mall attract more customers? shopping
Modern malls have to compete with e-commerce, so they need to offer what online shopping cannot. Successful malls increasingly function as lifestyle hubs — incorporating cinemas, gyms, art exhibits, kid-friendly spaces, and curated dining. Loyalty programmes, seasonal events, and Instagrammable interior design also help. Ultimately, the question isn't "how do we sell more products" but "how do we make people want to spend their afternoon here."
Q7. Why is it difficult for some people to relax? relaxation
Several factors. Modern work culture glorifies busyness, so people feel guilty when they're not being productive. Smartphones blur the boundary between work and leisure — emails follow us everywhere. And many people simply never learned how to relax; they don't have hobbies or restorative rituals, so when they finally have free time, they default to scrolling, which isn't truly restful. Genuine relaxation, ironically, is a skill that takes practice.
Q8. Which is more important, mental relaxation or physical relaxation? relaxation
In an ideal world, both — they're deeply intertwined. Physical relaxation through exercise, massage, or sleep helps regulate stress hormones, while mental relaxation through meditation or hobbies prevents burnout. If forced to choose, I'd lean slightly toward mental relaxation, because chronic mental stress can persist even when your body is resting. But genuinely, neglecting either creates a downstream problem in the other.
Q9. Should there be classes teaching young people how to relax? relaxation
Honestly, yes — and I'd argue it's overdue. Schools teach children plenty about productivity and discipline but almost nothing about recovery, mindfulness, and stress management. Given how common anxiety and burnout have become among young people, basic skills like breathing techniques, time off from screens, and healthy hobbies should be part of the curriculum. Teaching kids to relax is just as important as teaching them to work.

Part 3 · Bank 5 — Spending, Movies, Stories, Apps, Heirlooms, Jobs

Pairs with Part 2 Script 5 (Vintage Camera & Films)
Question topics covered: 花费超预期 · 观影 · 家庭重要东西 · 故事 · App · 离不开 · 完美工作 · 失望电影
Q1. How do advertisements influence people's spending habits? spending
Advertisements are remarkably effective at manufacturing desire. They don't just inform — they create emotional associations, linking products with happiness, status, or identity. Modern targeted ads are even more potent because algorithms know our browsing behaviour intimately and serve up precisely what we're vulnerable to. The result is that people often buy things they didn't know they wanted ten minutes earlier. The defence is media literacy and a 24-hour rule before any non-essential purchase.
Q2. Is it important to save money? Why? spending
Saving is absolutely essential in modern life. It provides a financial cushion against emergencies — medical bills, job loss, sudden moves — and gives you the freedom to make choices based on values rather than desperation. Saving also enables long-term goals like education, homeownership, or retirement. Without savings, you're trapped in a paycheck-to-paycheck cycle where every setback becomes a crisis.
Q3. What are the factors that make a successful movie? movies
Several factors converge. A compelling story with relatable characters is always the foundation — without that, no amount of effects can save a film. Strong direction, smart casting, and high production values reinforce the story. Marketing and timing matter too — even great films can flop if released against blockbusters. Increasingly, cultural resonance is critical: films that tap into the current zeitgeist tend to dominate the box office.
Q4. Why do people prefer to watch movies in the cinema? movies
The cinema offers an immersive experience that home viewing can't fully replicate — a giant screen, surround sound, and the absence of distractions force you to engage fully with the film. There's also a social dimension: laughing or gasping with a hundred strangers creates a kind of communal energy. And going to the cinema is itself a ritual — buying popcorn, dimming the lights — that elevates the act of watching into an event rather than background noise.
Q5. Do you believe movie reviews? movies
Selectively. I trust reviews from critics whose taste aligns with mine over time, and I also pay attention to audience-aggregate scores rather than individual reviews, which can be biased. However, I'm cautious of paid promotional reviews and overly hyped early opinions, which often don't reflect the film's true quality. Honestly, my best filter is friends whose taste I know — that's far more reliable than any professional reviewer.
Q6. Why are bedtime stories important for children? stories
Bedtime stories serve multiple functions at once. They build vocabulary and listening skills at a critical developmental stage, foster a love of reading, and create a calming bonding ritual between parent and child. Stories also help children process emotions and moral concepts safely — through fictional characters, they explore fear, kindness, and consequences. And on the practical side, the predictable rhythm of a nightly story helps signal to the brain that it's time to sleep.
Q7. Are traditional stories still relevant for children today? stories
Many of them absolutely are, though selectively. Traditional fairy tales carry timeless themes — courage, kindness, the consequences of greed — that remain universally relevant. They also connect children to cultural heritage. That said, some old stories contain outdated gender roles or troubling messages that need careful reinterpretation. The healthiest approach is to share them alongside modern stories, helping children engage critically with the values inside.
Q8. Do you think mobile apps have made our lives easier or more complicated? apps
Genuinely both. On the convenience side, apps have streamlined countless daily tasks — banking, navigation, shopping, communication. On the complexity side, we now juggle dozens of apps, each demanding attention through notifications, and the cognitive load of constant context-switching is real. So while apps make individual tasks faster, the cumulative effect on our attention and mental clarity is arguably negative. Healthy use requires deliberate digital hygiene.
Q9. Why is it important for children to learn about family history? heirlooms
Knowing your family history gives children a sense of identity and continuity — they understand they're part of a larger story rather than isolated individuals. Studies have actually shown that children who know more about their family history tend to be more emotionally resilient, possibly because that sense of belonging anchors them during hard times. It also fosters intergenerational connection and helps preserve cultural traditions that might otherwise be lost.
Q10. Will people keep fewer physical objects in the future as memories? heirlooms
Almost certainly, yes. Photos, music, books, and even letters have been progressively dematerialised over the past two decades. The next generation may have very few physical mementos compared to their grandparents. There's a trade-off here: digital storage is more compact and shareable, but physical objects carry a sensory weight — texture, smell, patina — that no JPEG can replicate. I suspect future generations will increasingly treasure rare physical artefacts precisely because they're rare.
Q11. What should people consider when choosing a job? jobs
Several dimensions matter. Salary obviously, but also growth potential, work-life balance, and company culture. Increasingly, people also consider purpose — does the work align with their values? Geography matters too, especially for remote vs in-office roles. The best framework I've seen is to ask: "Will I learn here, will I be paid fairly, and will I be respected?" If the answer to all three is yes, it's probably worth pursuing, even if no individual factor is perfect.
Q12. Is salary the main reason people choose a job? jobs
For some, yes — particularly those supporting families or paying off debt. But research consistently shows that beyond a certain income threshold, salary contributes surprisingly little to long-term happiness. Factors like meaningful work, supportive colleagues, autonomy, and growth opportunities matter far more for sustained job satisfaction. So salary is a strong initial filter, but it's rarely the deepest reason people stay in or leave a role.
Q13. What is more important for job satisfaction: high salary or good relationships with colleagues? jobs
In the long run, good relationships almost always win. A high salary stops feeling exciting fairly quickly — it's what economists call "hedonic adaptation." But a toxic workplace can drain you every single day, regardless of how much you're paid. Conversely, supportive colleagues can make even mediocre work feel meaningful. Of course, an extremely low salary is its own kind of stressor, so there's a baseline. But beyond that baseline, relationships are decisive.
